01

What is actually being graded right now

The thread optimised almost entirely for a Grand Finale demo. That is the third gate, not the first. Two gates come before it, and both are judged on the deck alone.

The internal college hackathon decides who gets nominated at all — most teams are eliminated here, and the SPOC nominates on the 6-slide format. Then national Round 1 scores the submitted idea on five parameters, each out of 20: presentation of the solution, innovation, solution approach, technical soundness and feasibility, and execution timeline for the hackathon — explicitly "blueprint of prototype completion timeline with respect to hackathon duration". Round 1 carries 20% of the final score.

The deck currently in the SIH folder has no timeline anywhere on it. That is a scored parameter left blank, and it is the cheapest fix on this page.

02

Faults, ranked

Severity is judged by one test: what happens if an NCMRWF evaluator notices it during the pitch or Q&A.

F1

The competitor analysis missed MoES's own flood warning systems

Critical

The thread listed Google Flood Hub, IBM, Tomorrow.io, Esri, IMD Mausam, SACHET, NRSC atlases and BMC's GIS — and concluded "no system couples rainfall nowcasts with drainage hydraulics for Indian cities". Both statements are wrong in the worst possible direction. MoES built CFLOWS-Chennai (launched 2019, run by NCCR with IMD, NCMRWF and INCOIS inputs) and iFLOWS-Mumbai (launched June 2020, with MCGM). iFLOWS runs a coupled 1D drainage + 2D hydrodynamic model, on a 20 cm DEM supplied by MCGM and 120 rain gauges, and issues ward-level water depth 6 to 72 hours ahead.

NCMRWF is a named contributor to both. Claiming novelty against a gap the evaluators personally filled is how a promising deck dies in ninety seconds.

PIB release 11 Jun 2020; NCCR iFLOWS and CFLOWS briefing documents.

F2

The rainfall input cannot support the claim built on top of it

Critical

The thread's recommendation is to skip radar and drive the model from IMD's district nowcast API. That product is district-scale — one categorical warning covering thousands of square kilometres. You cannot force a street-level, centimetre-band inundation model with it and call the result a nowcast. This, not the drainage graph, is the weakest link in the proposed architecture, and it sits in front of the one department that actually specialises in nowcasting.

It also overstates the block: IMD's public API index does list a radar image product and lightning data alongside the district and station nowcasts, the national radar mosaic is published openly, and raw DWR volume data is orderable through IMD's Data Supply Portal. Radar-based nowcasting is not off the table.

api.imd.gov.in API reference; mausam.imd.gov.in radar services; radarapi.imd.gov.in.

F3

Planned for a 48-hour hackathon that does not exist

Major

Whole sections are built around "48 hours is the minimum viable" with an hour-by-hour 48-hour plan. The SIH Grand Finale is a 36-hour non-stop sprint at a nodal centre. Since Round 1 explicitly scores the build timeline against hackathon duration, submitting a 48-hour plan is a self-inflicted mark deduction and signals the team has not read the rules.

F4

Fake resolution: 30 m DEM "downscaled" to 10 m

Major

Bilinear interpolation of SRTM invents no terrain. A 30 m grid cannot resolve curbs, road crowns or the half-metre depressions that decide where a street ponds — and NRSC's policy is explicit: 30 m CartoDEM is the free tier, 10 m is chargeable and clearance-gated. Meanwhile iFLOWS runs on 20 cm survey data. Presenting "10 m interpolated" as a resolution is the single easiest thing for a judge to disassemble.

F5

The drainage data is treated as absent when it is partly public

Major

The thread repeatedly falls back to a "synthetic 10–20 node graph". OpenCity publishes Greater Chennai Corporation's stormwater drain maps: per-ward PDFs for 114 wards and a citywide 2023 KML of about 27 MB, public domain. That is real drain geometry — actual alignments and connectivity, not a guess. What is genuinely missing is attributes: diameter, invert level, slope, condition.

This changes the story you tell. "We synthesised a fake network" is a weakness; "we built the graph from GCC's published drain alignments and estimated the hydraulic attributes, flagged as estimated" is a method.

data.opencity.in — Chennai Stormwater Drain (SWD) Maps, source gcc.gov.in.

F6

A fabricated citation, repeated three times

Major

The validation and Q&A sections quote a historical flood report reading "Ward 52水深 1m". Chinese characters inside a supposed Chennai news quote mean the source does not exist. It is used as the ground truth for the entire validation plan, and the prompt that produced it explicitly forbade inventing sources. Anything downstream of it must be re-sourced from scratch.

F7

Invented figures, presented as answers to judges

Major

Scripted Q&A answers include cloud cost of "about ₹50k/month for 100 cities" and licensing at "₹10–20 lakh/year for a city", plus "₹1000+ crore/year" in Mumbai losses. None are sourced. Delivering an unsourced rupee figure to a government evaluator invites exactly one follow-up: "based on what?" Give a method instead of a number, or drop the claim.

F8

Broken repository reference

Minor

SWMManywhere is cited as github.com/Karney/SWMManywhere. The real project is ImperialCollegeLondon/SWMManywhere — BSD-3, pip-installable, JOSS-reviewed, and it synthesises a full SWMM model anywhere from a bounding box using OSM, DEM and building footprints. It is arguably the most useful tool in the whole thread, and the link given goes nowhere.

F9

The deck cites an unofficial mirror as its problem-statement source

Minor

Slide 6 links sih2026.vuce.in/ps/SIH26085. That site describes itself as a community archive "not affiliated with Smart India Hackathon or the Government of India". Citing a third-party scrape of the organiser's own portal, in a submission to that organiser, is a needless own-goal. Use sih.gov.in/sih2026PS.

F10

Geography errors and scoring theatre

Minor

"Mumbai Ward 116" appears in the executive summary as a demo target; Mumbai has 24 administrative wards, lettered A to T — 116 is a Chennai ward number that leaked across. Separately, the "7.2/10" verdict and "65–75% probability of a convincing prototype" are precision without a rubric. Both are noise; neither should reach a slide.

F11

The GNN is placed first among differentiators, and it is the one you may not finish

Minor

A graph surrogate for drainage hydraulics is real, published work and worth pursuing — but it needs thousands of SWMM runs generated as training data before it does anything. The thread ranks it as the top innovation and then, in its own Q&A script, has you telling judges "we use a pre-trained GNN on synthetic SWMM data". If the surrogate is not trained, that sentence is untrue. Rank it as an accelerator, not the headline.

F12

A whole turn spent on the wrong repository

Minor

The FloodSense evaluation reached the right answer — not a defensible baseline — but it assessed a repo it admitted it could never load, from Devpost and a journal paper that contradict each other. The verdict stands; just keep FloodSense out of the deck entirely rather than citing it as related work.

03

The workarounds

Reposition before you rebuild. The strongest sentence available to this team is not "nobody does this" — it is: MoES already warns Mumbai and Chennai at ward scale, 6 to 72 hours ahead, using bespoke survey data that took years and cannot be repeated for every Indian city. We nowcast the next three hours at street-segment scale, from open data, in a ward we can stand up anywhere in a day.

That is true, it is checkable, and it flatters the evaluators' own work instead of denying it.

Fixes F1

Put iFLOWS and CFLOWS on the slide yourself

A three-row comparison — lead time, spatial unit, data dependency — with iFLOWS, CFLOWS and you. Naming the incumbent first proves you did the homework and removes the ambush from Q&A.

The gap you own: 0–3 h, street segment, open data, re-runnable in seconds, exposed as a routing API. Never claim to beat iFLOWS on accuracy.

Fixes F2

Make the rainfall nowcast real, in two tiers

Tier 1: optical-flow advection of successive public radar reflectivity frames to produce a 0–180 min rainfall field at radar pixel scale. This is a classical, defensible nowcasting method and it is the component NCMRWF will judge hardest.

Tier 2 fallback: IMD district nowcast plus AWS/ARG station rainfall from the public API to set magnitude, clearly labelled as coarse forcing. Verify tier 1 against later observed frames using CSI and lead time.

Fixes F5, F8

Build the drainage graph from GCC's own KML

Load the 2023 citywide SWD KML, clip to one ward, and derive nodes and directed edges from the real alignments. Attach attributes in two classes and colour them differently in the UI: measured where the ward PDF gives a size, estimated where you infer from drain class and DEM slope.

Use SWMManywhere to generate a SWMM model for any second ward or city, and PySWMM to run it. That is your portability proof.

Fixes F4

Stop selling grid resolution, sell the unit of decision

Predict per road segment and per drainage node, not per 10 m pixel. Segments come from OSM, relative elevation and slope from 30 m CartoDEM or SRTM, and the model reports a depth band and a time-to-flood per segment.

This is honest about the terrain data, and it is also the form a traffic controller or an ambulance dispatcher can act on.

Fixes F6

Rebuild validation on events you can actually document

Pick two events with public traces — Chennai December 2015 and Cyclone Michaung December 2023 are the obvious pair — and assemble a list of streets reported waterlogged from GCC complaint data and dated news reports with locations you can geocode yourself.

Then score the binary question the PS actually asks: did this segment flood? Report POD, FAR and CSI with the confusion matrix visible. A confusion matrix on twenty streets beats an invented 95%.

Fixes F3

Re-cut every plan to 36 hours

And put a compact version of it on the feasibility slide, because Round 1 scores it. Hours 0–4 data and skeleton, 4–12 coupled model on one ward, 12–20 API and map, 20–26 routing, 26–31 scenarios and validation table, 31–36 hardening, rehearsal, recorded backup demo.

Fixes F7, F10, F11

Delete every unsourced number

No rupee figures, no accuracy percentages, no probability of success. Where a number is genuinely needed, show the method that would produce it. Mark model performance as a target until measured, which the current deck already does well in one place — make it consistent everywhere.

04

Where the win actually is

Twenty other teams will show a map with red streets on it. Three things separate a shortlisted deck from those, and none of them is the model architecture.

LeverWhat it looks like in the demoWhy it scores
The judge's knob Two live controls: a rainfall multiplier, and click any manhole to block it to 50% capacity. Map, depth bands and the ambulance route all recompute in front of them. It is the one demand that separates a simulation from a recorded video, and the thread was right that judges ask it. Design for it from hour zero — nothing precomputed.
The causal panel Click a flagged street: inflow at the contributing inlets, pipe capacity by Manning, the node that surcharges, the minute it happens, and the confidence class of every attribute used. Turns "the AI says red" into an engineering argument. This is what makes a student build read as government-grade, and it is cheap to implement.
Portability in one command Enter a bounding box for a ward the judges name — including one you have never seen — and generate a runnable drainage model live from open data. iFLOWS took years of bathymetry and survey per city. If you can bootstrap a rough ward model anywhere in minutes, you address the cities MoES will never survey. This is your real USP.

Two smaller things worth the effort: name a real deployment partner ambition on the impact slide rather than a generic stakeholder list, and add uncertainty as a first-class output — a segment flagged "high risk, low confidence" is more credible than a false precision, and it is the honest consequence of estimated pipe attributes.

06

Questions to be ready for

MoES already runs iFLOWS-Mumbai and CFLOWS-Chennai. What are you adding?

Lead time and spatial unit. iFLOWS forecasts 6 to 72 hours ahead at ward level, from a 20 cm survey DEM and a dedicated gauge network. We target 0 to 3 hours at road-segment level, driven by radar advection rather than NWP, and we run on open data so the same pipeline can be stood up in a city that has no survey programme. We are complementary to iFLOWS, not a replacement for it — the natural production form is a nowcast module feeding the same dissemination channel.

Where does your rainfall actually come from, and at what resolution?

Successive public radar reflectivity frames, advected forward by optical flow to 180 minutes, converted to rain rate with a standard Z–R relation whose coefficients we state. We verify the advection against the observed frames that follow, and report CSI by lead time. When radar is unavailable we fall back to district nowcast plus station rainfall, and the dashboard labels the run as coarse forcing — the forecast does not silently degrade.

Your DEM is 30 m. How can you predict street-level water depth?

We do not predict a depth field at 30 m and call it street level. We predict per road segment and per drainage node: relative elevation and slope from the DEM, contributing area from land cover, and the surcharge state from the drainage model. Output is a depth band with a confidence class, never a centimetre point value. Where a city has LiDAR or survey terrain, the same model consumes it and the bands tighten.

Is your drainage network real?

The geometry is: alignments and connectivity come from the Greater Chennai Corporation stormwater drain maps published on OpenCity. The hydraulic attributes are not — diameters, inverts and slopes are estimated from drain class and terrain, and the interface marks every estimated attribute. In production these come from the corporation's asset database. We would rather show you which parameters are estimated than present a network we cannot substantiate.

How do you know any of this is right?

We replay a real event and score a binary question: did this segment flood. Ground truth is the list of streets documented as waterlogged in corporation complaint records and dated reports for that event. We report POD, FAR and CSI with the confusion matrix, on a stated number of segments. It is a small sample and we say so. We do not claim a depth accuracy, because we have no depth observations to claim it against.

What did you build yourselves, and what did an AI write?

Worth rehearsing honestly — 2026 panels ask it. The defensible split: tooling, UI scaffolding and data plumbing were AI-accelerated; the coupling logic, the parameter estimation, the verification protocol and every number on the slides are ours, and any member can derive the runoff and pipe-capacity equations at the whiteboard. Make sure that last part is true before you say it.

08

Appendix A — FloodSense, in plain language

The Perplexity thread judged this repository without ever loading it. We downloaded the code and read every file. Here is what it actually is, with no jargon.

It is a phone app. About 2,700 lines of Dart across 17 screens, built with Flutter, uploaded in a single commit on 12 November 2025, and free to reuse under an MIT licence. As a student phone app it is competent: it has sign-up, a map, photo uploads, and a way for people to report flooding they see. As a starting point for SIH26085 it is the wrong species of software — the problem statement asks for a server that runs a water model and a web dashboard, and there is no server and no model here.

The featureWhat the code really doesWhy that is not the problem
"Flood detection" Adds up all the rain in the weather service's five-day forecast. If the total goes over 100 mm, it prints "High Flood Risk". It is a rain total with a threshold — no ground, no slope, no drains, and no sense of when. Heavy rain spread over five days is harmless; the same rain in forty minutes floods a city.
"Flood zone map" Draws one circle of 80 km radius around wherever you are standing, and colours it blue if the weather service has any flood warning for the region. An 80 km circle covers Chennai and most of the district around it. The problem statement asks which street corner floods.
"Safe route" Asks a routing service for three routes, then checks the regional weather warning at points spaced 1 km apart along each one. Because the warning is regional, every point on every route returns the same answer — so all three routes are safe together or risky together. The app never tells the router to avoid anywhere; it just hides routes.
"Crowdsourced reports" People submit a flood sighting with a photo; stored in a cloud database. This part genuinely works. It is a decent idea and the only part worth thinking about later — but it is an add-on, not the coupled rainfall-and-drainage model being asked for.

Three things you would inherit by forking it. Keys left in the code: a weather-service key sits in plain text in two files, a live database key in a third, and the Google services file is committed — fork it and someone else's credentials are in your project's history. It does not run as published: the routing screen reads a settings file that the app never loads and never packages, so a fresh copy crashes the moment you open the headline feature. No real tests: the only test file is the template Flutter generates, still carrying its "change this matcher" comment.

Verdict: do not build on it. Take one idea — showing three routes, marking each one's risk, and making the user confirm before taking a flagged one is a sound interaction worth copying when you design your routing panel. Write it yourself; do not import the file, and do not cite the project in the deck.

The real danger is not code quality. Starting from a phone app quietly pulls the team towards building a citizen app, when SIH26085 asks for a web dashboard and an API that navigation systems can call. That detour costs weeks you do not have.

Four parallel research passes across GitHub, government portals and the open literature. Everything listed below was opened and checked; where something could not be verified it is marked as such. The rule is the same as everywhere else on this page: no repository is recommended because its name sounds relevant.

Two rival SIH26085 demos are already public

Both are live right now and both self-identify as SIH26085 entries. One is a Mumbai street map with a 0–180 minute time scrubber and a real simulate-and-replay backend; its own footer admits synthetic rainfall and an inferred drainage network, and it reports street depths above 200 cm that will not survive scrutiny. The other is a polished dashboard built on an invented ward with invented street names, with sample scores displayed and honestly labelled as illustrative, drawing live rainfall from a public weather API "as a demo substitute for a Doppler radar nowcast feed".

That is the bar. Both are prettier than anything you will build in two weeks — and both are hollow at exactly the two points that decide this problem statement. A team that plugs in real drain geometry and reports measured skill on a real event beats both without needing a nicer interface.

What to skip, and why. The GNN drainage surrogate the thread ranked first — Zhiyu014/GNN-UDS — pins TensorFlow 2.6 and a 2021-era stack that will fight a modern Python install; treat the idea as a slide, not a dependency. Deep-learning rainfall nowcasters (DGMR, SmaAt-UNet, NowcastNet) either ship no usable weights or are trained on European radar grids, and claiming an uncalibrated one to nowcasting specialists is a liability. Compiled or GPU-only flood solvers (ANUGA, BG_Flood) will eat the build in compilation. And OSRM, despite being the obvious routing name, precomputes its graph — you cannot change edge costs per request, which is precisely what a nowcast needs.
